# Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP)

## Overview
Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is the use of antiretroviral medication by HIV-negative people to prevent HIV infection. When taken correctly, PrEP is highly effective (over 99% for sexual transmission) at preventing HIV.

## What PrEP Is
- A daily medication (TDF + FTC or TDF + 3TC) that prevents HIV from establishing infection after exposure.
- PrEP contains two antiretroviral drugs (tenofovir + emtricitabine or lamivudine) — the same drugs used in ART.
- PrEP is for HIV-negative people only. Do NOT take PrEP if you have HIV — it is not adequate treatment for HIV.
- In Nigeria, PrEP is available in public health facilities and some private clinics, often through PEPFAR-supported programmes.

## Who Is Eligible for PrEP

PrEP should be offered to any HIV-negative person at **substantial risk of HIV infection:**

### Sexual Risk Factors
- HIV-positive sexual partner (especially if partner has detectable viral load or is not on ART)
- Multiple sexual partners with unknown HIV status
- Inconsistent or no condom use with casual partners
- History of STIs in the past 6 months
- Sex work or transactional sex
- Sexual partner(s) from key populations (MSM, people who inject drugs, sex workers)
- History of taking PEP (especially repeated courses)

### Injection Drug Use
- Sharing needles or syringes
- Injection partner living with HIV

### Adolescent Girls and Young Women (AGYW) in Nigeria
- PrEP is especially recommended for AGYW in high-prevalence areas (e.g., Akwa Ibom, Rivers, Benue, Lagos, FCT)
- AGYW face higher HIV incidence due to biological vulnerability and structural factors

### Key Populations
- Men who have sex with men (MSM)
- Female sex workers (FSW)
- People who inject drugs (PWID)
- Transgender people

## How to Start PrEP

### Step 1: Confirm HIV-Negative Status
- An HIV rapid test must be done and confirmed negative **on the same day** before starting PrEP.
- If the test is reactive (positive), do not start PrEP — refer for ART.

### Step 2: Baseline Tests
- HIV test (mandatory)
- Serum creatinine / renal function test (to ensure kidneys are healthy — TDF is cleared through kidneys)
- Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) — TDF/FTC treats HBV, which is fine, but needs monitoring
- Hepatitis C test (if indicated)
- Pregnancy test (for women of childbearing age — not a contraindication)

### Step 3: Counselling
- How to take PrEP daily
- Importance of adherence
- Follow-up schedule (3-monthly HIV testing and refills)
- PrEP does not protect against other STIs or pregnancy

## PrEP Regimens

### Standard Daily PrEP
- **Truvada (TDF 300 mg + FTC 200 mg):** One tablet once daily. Most studied and WHO-recommended.
- **Generic TDF + 3TC:** Commonly used in Nigeria (lamivudine instead of emtricitabine; both are effective).
- Must be taken daily for at least **7 days** in women and **21 days** in men who have receptive anal sex for maximum protection.
- Start PrEP at least 7 days before potential exposure for vaginal sex; 21 days for receptive anal sex.

### Event-Driven PrEP (ED-PrEP, "2-1-1")
Only for cisgender men who have sex with men (MSM) who have infrequent sex:
- **Before sex (2 pills):** 2–24 hours before sex
- **During sex (if continuing):** 1 pill 24 hours after the first dose
- **After sex (1 pill):** 1 pill 24 hours after the second dose

**Important:** ED-PrEP is NOT recommended for women, heterosexual men, or people who inject drugs. It requires good planning and is not for everyone.

## Effectiveness
- **>99% effective** for sexual transmission when taken consistently.
- Effectiveness is directly proportional to adherence:
  - Daily dosing: >99%
  - 4+ doses per week: ~96%
  - 2–3 doses per week: ~76%
  - <2 doses per week: negligible protection
- For injection drug use: ~74–84% effective when taken 4+ times per week.

## Monitoring Schedule

| Time Point | Tests Needed |
|------------|-------------|
| **Baseline** | HIV test, creatinine, HBsAg, pregnancy test |
| **Month 1** | HIV test, adherence check, side effect review |
| **Every 3 months** | HIV test, creatinine (if baseline abnormal or high risk), adherence counselling, STI screening |
| **Every 6–12 months** | Creatinine (routine renal monitoring) |

### Stopping PrEP
- If HIV test becomes reactive (positive) — stop PrEP immediately and transition to ART.
- If the person is no longer at substantial risk — PrEP can be stopped safely.
- When stopping, take the last dose, and continue using condoms for 28 days after the last dose.
- PrEP does not cause a "rebound" or withdrawal effect.

## Side Effects

### Common (usually resolve in first week)
- Nausea (most common) — take with food
- Diarrhoea
- Headache
- Dizziness
- Fatigue
- Flatulence

These usually resolve within 1–2 weeks. If they persist, the person can be switched to TAF-based PrEP (where available).

### Serious (rare)
- **Renal toxicity:** Small increase in serum creatinine (~2–5% of users). Reversible after stopping TDF. TAF has less renal toxicity.
- **Bone density loss:** Small decrease in bone mineral density with TDF. Clinical fractures are rare.
- **Lactic acidosis:** Very rare.

## PrEP vs PEP

| Feature | PrEP | PEP |
|---------|------|-----|
| **Purpose** | Pre-exposure prevention | Post-exposure emergency |
| **Timing** | Before potential exposure | Within 72 hours of exposure |
| **Duration** | Ongoing (daily) | 28 days only |
| **HIV status** | Negative | Negative |
| **Effectiveness** | >99% with daily use | High but not 100% |
| **Who needs it** | People at ongoing substantial risk | Anyone with recent high-risk exposure |

## Key Considerations for Adolescents
- PrEP is safe for adolescents (studies show good adherence and effectiveness).
- Adolescents need youth-friendly services and confidential access.
- Adherence can be challenging — support systems (peer navigators, phone reminders) are important.
- Nigerian guidelines recommend PrEP for adolescents aged 15+ at substantial risk.

## PrEP in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding
- PrEP is safe during pregnancy and breastfeeding.
- TDF/FTC has not been associated with adverse birth outcomes.
- Pregnancy is a time of high HIV risk — PrEP should be offered to HIV-negative pregnant women at substantial risk.
- Both WHO and Nigeria's guidelines support PrEP use in pregnancy and breastfeeding.

## Key Counselling Points
- PrEP prevents HIV, not other STIs or pregnancy — use condoms too.
- You must take PrEP daily — missing doses reduces protection.
- You must test for HIV every 3 months — do not refill PrEP without a negative test.
- PrEP is safe and effective — side effects are usually mild and go away.
- PrEP does not cause birth defects — it is safe during pregnancy and breastfeeding.
- PrEP is available in Nigeria — ask at your local health facility or HIV clinic.
- Starting PrEP does not mean you will have sex — it is your choice. PrEP gives you control over your HIV risk.
