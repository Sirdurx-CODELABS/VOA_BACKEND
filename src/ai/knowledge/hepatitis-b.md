# Hepatitis B

## Overview
Hepatitis B is a viral infection that attacks the liver. It is caused by the hepatitis B virus (HBV). HBV is 50–100 times more infectious than HIV. Chronic hepatitis B can lead to cirrhosis (liver scarring) and liver cancer (hepatocellular carcinoma). Co-infection with HIV is common and requires coordinated treatment.

## Transmission
HBV is transmitted through:
- **Blood:** Sharing needles, syringes, razors, toothbrushes; blood transfusion (rare where screening is done)
- **Sexual contact:** Unprotected vaginal, anal, or oral sex
- **Mother-to-child:** During childbirth (perinatal transmission) — the most common route in countries like Nigeria where HBV is endemic
- **Household contact:** Open cuts, sores, sharing sharp objects

HBV can survive outside the body for at least 7 days and still cause infection if it enters a person who is not immune.

## Testing for Hepatitis B

### Who Should Be Tested
- All people living with HIV (routine screening at HIV diagnosis)
- All pregnant women (first antenatal visit)
- People with abnormal liver function tests
- Sexual partners of people with hepatitis B
- People who inject drugs
- People with multiple sexual partners
- Healthcare workers (before and after HBV vaccination)

### Tests

| Test | What It Tells |
|------|---------------|
| **HBsAg** (Hepatitis B surface antigen) | Presence of active infection (positive = currently infected) |
| **Anti-HBs** (Antibody to HBsAg) | Immunity (positive = protected, from vaccination or past infection) |
| **Anti-HBc** (Antibody to core antigen) | Past or present infection (positive = has been or is infected) |
| **HBeAg** | High viral replication and infectivity |
| **HBV DNA** | Viral load — measures active replication; guides treatment decisions |
| **Liver function tests (LFTs)** | Assess liver damage (ALT, AST) |
| **Liver ultrasound** | Monitor for cirrhosis and liver cancer |

## Co-Infection with HIV
- An estimated 5–20% of people with HIV in Nigeria also have chronic hepatitis B (prevalence varies by region).
- HBV co-infection accelerates liver disease progression in people with HIV.
- HIV can worsen HBV reactivation.
- All people with HIV should be screened for HBV at diagnosis.

### Treatment of HIV/HBV Co-Infection
- **ART is the cornerstone of HBV treatment in co-infected patients.**
- Preferred ART regimen: **TDF (or TAF) + 3TC (or FTC) + DTG** — because TDF and 3TC are active against both HIV and HBV.
  - TDF and TAF: Potent against HBV; TAF has less renal and bone toxicity.
  - 3TC/FTC: Active against both viruses.
- Stopping TDF or 3TC/FTC in a co-infected patient can cause severe HBV flare — **never interrupt HBV-active drugs** if HBV is present.
- Regular monitoring: HBV DNA, LFTs, alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), liver ultrasound every 6–12 months.
- Vaccinate all people with HIV who are HBV-negative against HBV.

## Treatment (for HBV Mono-Infection or Co-Infection)

### Goals
- Suppress HBV viral replication
- Reduce liver inflammation and fibrosis
- Prevent cirrhosis and liver cancer

### First-Line Drugs (Active Against HBV)
- **Tenofovir (TDF or TAF):** Potent, low resistance, given once daily
- **Entecavir:** Potent, low resistance, not active against HIV (do not use in HIV co-infection without ART)
- **Lamivudine (3TC):** Effective but has higher resistance rate; always use with another HBV-active drug
- **Pegylated interferon:** Finite course (48 weeks); used in selected cases

### Monitoring on Treatment
- HBV DNA every 6–12 months
- LFTs every 3–6 months
- Alpha-fetoprotein + liver ultrasound every 6–12 months (liver cancer screening)
- In HIV co-infection: HIV viral load and CD4 count per HIV guidelines

## Vaccination

### HBV Vaccine
- Highly effective (95% protection in healthy adults).
- Given in 3 doses: at 0, 1, and 6 months.
- Nigeria's national immunization programme includes HBV vaccine within the pentavalent vaccine (given at 6, 10, and 14 weeks).
- For adults (especially people with HIV, healthcare workers, contacts of HBV carriers): 3-dose series.
- After vaccination, test Anti-HBs to confirm immunity (≥10 mIU/mL = protective).
- People with HIV may have a lower response rate to HBV vaccine; use double-dose or 4-dose schedule in HIV if available.

### Who Should Be Vaccinated
- All infants (included in national immunization programme)
- All people with HIV who are HBsAg and anti-HBc negative
- Healthcare workers
- Sexual partners of HBsAg-positive people
- People who inject drugs
- People with chronic liver disease
- Travellers to endemic areas

## Chronic vs Acute Hepatitis B

### Acute (<6 months)
- Most adults (>90%) clear the virus spontaneously.
- Symptoms: fatigue, jaundice (yellow eyes/skin), dark urine, nausea, right upper quadrant pain.
- Treatment: supportive care; no specific antiviral needed unless severe.

### Chronic (>6 months)
- HBsAg positive for >6 months.
- Risk of chronic infection is:
  - >90% in newborns infected at birth
  - 20–50% in children infected at 1–5 years
  - <5% in adults infected
- Chronic carriers need lifelong monitoring and possible treatment.

## Prevention
- **Vaccination:** The single most effective prevention strategy.
- **Safe sex:** Condom use reduces HBV transmission.
- **Infection control:** Safe injection practices in healthcare; avoid sharing sharp objects, razors, toothbrushes.
- **Screening of blood products:** Mandatory in Nigerian blood banks.
- **PMTCT of HBV:** Screen all pregnant women; give HBV vaccine + HBIG (hepatitis B immune globulin) to infants of HBsAg-positive mothers within 12 hours of birth if available.
- **For HIV/HBV co-infected pregnant women:** Start ART containing TDF + 3TC/FTC for both maternal health and PMTCT of both HIV and HBV.

## Key Counselling Points
- Hepatitis B is a serious liver infection, but it is manageable with treatment.
- If you have both HIV and hepatitis B, your ART (containing TDF) treats both — never stop it.
- HBV is easily transmitted — get your household and sexual partners tested and vaccinated.
- Avoid alcohol — it worsens liver damage.
- Regular checkups (liver ultrasound, blood tests) are important to monitor your liver health.
- The HBV vaccine is safe and effective — make sure you and your family are vaccinated.
- There is no cure for chronic hepatitis B, but treatment keeps the virus suppressed and prevents liver damage.
