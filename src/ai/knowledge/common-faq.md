# Common FAQs — HIV, ART, and Sexual Health

## HIV Transmission

### 1. Can I get HIV from kissing?
No. HIV is not transmitted through saliva. Closed-mouth or open-mouth kissing poses no risk of HIV transmission. The virus cannot survive in saliva, and there is no documented case of HIV transmission through kissing.

### 2. Can I get HIV from mosquito bites?
No. Mosquitoes do not transmit HIV. HIV cannot survive or reproduce in insects. The virus dies quickly outside the human body. All studies confirm no HIV transmission from insect bites.

### 3. Can I get HIV from sharing a toilet seat?
No. HIV is not transmitted through shared toilet seats, bathwater, swimming pools, or any other casual contact. The virus cannot survive on surfaces for extended periods.

### 4. Can I get HIV from oral sex?
The risk of HIV transmission through oral sex is very low, but not zero. The risk increases if there are mouth sores, bleeding gums, or open cuts. Using condoms or dental dams during oral sex reduces the already-low risk further.

### 5. Can I get HIV from blood transfusion?
Very rare in Nigeria today. All blood products in Nigerian blood banks are screened for HIV. The risk is from unscreened blood (e.g., in emergencies, traditional settings, or unlicensed blood banks). Always ensure blood is screened before transfusion.

### 6. Is it true that having an STI increases my risk of getting HIV?
Yes. STIs cause inflammation and sores that make it easier for HIV to enter the body. An untreated STI can increase HIV acquisition risk by 2–5 times. Treating STIs is a key HIV prevention strategy.

### 7. Can I get HIV if I have sex with someone who looks healthy?
Yes. A person can have HIV and look completely healthy — especially if they were recently infected or are on ART. The only way to know someone's HIV status is through testing.

## HIV Testing

### 8. How long after exposure should I test for HIV?
- **4th-generation test (antigen/antibody):** 4 weeks (99% accurate)
- **Rapid test (antibody only):** 6 weeks to 3 months
- **PCR test (for infants):** 2–3 weeks
Test at 6 weeks and confirm at 3 months for reliable results.

### 9. Is HIV testing confidential?
Yes. In Nigeria, healthcare providers are required to keep your HIV test result confidential. Test results cannot be shared with anyone (including family, employers, or school) without your written consent.

### 10. Do I need my parents' consent to get an HIV test?
If you are 16 years or older, you can consent to HIV testing without a parent or guardian in Nigeria. If you are under 16, parental/guardian consent is generally required, but mature minors may be able to test independently in some settings.

### 11. Can HIV be cured if caught early?
No. There is no cure for HIV — not even if caught early. However, starting ART early gives you the best chance of staying healthy. ART suppresses the virus but does not eliminate it. Only a handful of cases (e.g., the "Berlin Patient," "London Patient") have been cured through bone marrow transplant, which is not a practical treatment.

## HIV Treatment and ART

### 12. Is there a cure for HIV?
Currently, no widely available cure exists for HIV. ART is a life-long treatment that controls the virus but does not eliminate it. Research into a cure continues.

### 13. How long do I need to take ART?
For life. ART does not cure HIV — it keeps the virus suppressed. Stopping ART will cause the virus to rebound, the immune system to weaken, and HIV to progress.

### 14. Can I stop ART if I feel fine?
No. Feeling fine means the ART is working. If you stop, the virus will start multiplying again, your CD4 count will drop, and you will eventually become sick. You may also develop drug resistance, making future treatment harder.

### 15. What do I do if I miss a dose?
- **Same day (remember within 12 hours):** Take the missed dose immediately, then take your next dose at the usual time.
- **Next day (more than 12 hours):** Skip the missed dose. Take your regular dose at the usual time.
- **Multiple missed doses:** Restart with a single dose and contact your healthcare provider.
Do NOT double your dose to make up for a missed one.

### 16. Can I stop ART to take a "drug holiday"?
No. Drug holidays (stopping ART for a period of time) are dangerous — they allow the virus to multiply, damage the immune system, and increase the risk of resistance. Never stop ART without your doctor's advice.

### 17. Can I drink alcohol on ART?
Moderate alcohol consumption does not directly interact with most ART (including TLD). However, excessive alcohol can:
- Cause you to forget to take your medication
- Affect liver function (especially with EFV or PIs)
- Lead to poor adherence and risky sexual behaviour
The safest approach: limit alcohol and take your ART every day.

### 18. Can I take herbal remedies with ART?
Many herbal remedies (including African traditional medicines) can interact with ART, reducing their effectiveness. St. John's wort, garlic supplements, and some immune boosters are known to interact. Always tell your healthcare provider about any herbal/traditional medicines you are taking.

### 19. What are the common side effects of ART?
Common side effects (usually resolve in 2–4 weeks): nausea, diarrhoea, headache, dizziness, fatigue, insomnia.
For DTG specifically: insomnia, weight gain, headache.
For EFV: nightmares, dizziness, drowsiness.
These are manageable — do not stop ART because of side effects.

### 20. I gained weight after starting DTG. Is that normal?
Yes. Weight gain is a known effect of DTG (dolutegravir). It is a sign that your body is recovering, but some people gain more than expected. Eat a balanced diet and stay active. If weight gain is concerning, talk to your provider.

## U=U

### 21. What is U=U?
U=U stands for **Undetectable = Untransmittable**. When a person living with HIV has a sustained undetectable viral load (<50 copies/mL), they cannot transmit HIV to their sexual partners. This is proven by large studies (PARTNER 1 & 2, HPTN 052, Opposites Attract).

### 22. Does U=U mean I can have sex without a condom?
If you are undetectable, you cannot transmit HIV to your sexual partner. However, condoms still protect against other STIs and pregnancy. The choice is yours.

### 23. How long do I need to be undetectable before U=U applies?
You need to have a sustained undetectable viral load (at least 6 months of confirmed undetectable results) before relying on U=U. A single undetectable result is not enough.

## HIV and Pregnancy

### 24. Can I have children if I am HIV positive?
Yes. Many people living with HIV have healthy, HIV-negative children. With effective ART throughout pregnancy, safe delivery, and proper infant feeding, the risk of transmission to the baby is less than 5% (as low as 1–2%).

### 25. Can I breastfeed if I am HIV positive?
Yes, if you are on ART and have an undetectable viral load. WHO and Nigeria recommend exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months while the mother is on ART. The transmission risk through breast milk is less than 1% with ART. Formula feeding is an alternative if it is safe, affordable, and sustainable.

### 26. Can I get pregnant while on PrEP?
Being on PrEP does not affect your ability to get pregnant or harm your baby. PrEP is safe during pregnancy and breastfeeding. If you become pregnant while on PrEP, continue taking it unless your provider advises otherwise.

## Disclosure and Relationships

### 27. Do I have to tell my partner I have HIV?
Legally in Nigeria, some states have laws requiring disclosure to sexual partners (e.g., Lagos State HIV Anti-Discrimination Law). Beyond legal requirements, disclosure is important for prevention and support. If you are struggling with how to tell your partner, ask for disclosure counselling at your clinic.

### 28. Can two people with HIV have sex without a condom?
Yes, but there are considerations. If both are on ART and have undetectable viral loads, the risk of HIV re-infection (superinfection) is extremely low. However, condoms protect against other STIs. Discuss with your partner and provider.

### 29. Can I date someone if I have HIV?
Yes. People living with HIV have relationships and get married. ART and U=U mean you can have a healthy sex life without transmitting HIV. Honest communication, disclosure, and mutual support are important.

## Children and HIV

### 30. Can a child born with HIV live a normal life?
Yes. With early diagnosis, prompt initiation of ART, and good adherence, children with HIV can grow up healthy, attend school, and live into adulthood. Many children on ART have undetectable viral loads and normal immune systems.

### 31. Should my child's school be told about their HIV status?
It depends. In general, there is no medical or legal requirement to tell the school. Disclosure should only happen if it directly benefits the child (e.g., if they need help with taking medication at school). Consider the risk of stigma and discrimination.

## Life with HIV

### 32. How long can a person with HIV live?
With early diagnosis, good adherence to ART, and regular medical care, a person with HIV can have a near-normal life expectancy. Many people diagnosed with HIV today will live into their 70s and 80s.

### 33. Can I exercise if I have HIV?
Yes. Exercise is beneficial for everyone, including people with HIV. Regular exercise improves cardiovascular health, bone density, mental health, and body composition (especially important if experiencing DTG-related weight gain).

### 34. Can I travel abroad if I have HIV?
Yes, but check the destination country's requirements. Most countries (including the UK, USA, Canada, most EU countries) have no restrictions. Some countries (e.g., Russia, UAE, Qatar) restrict entry or stay for people with HIV. Many require HIV test results for long-stay visas. Check before you travel.

### 35. What vaccines should I get if I have HIV?
Recommended vaccines for people with HIV:
- Influenza (yearly)
- COVID-19
- Pneumococcal (PCV13, PPSV23)
- Hepatitis B
- HPV (for those aged 9–45)
- Tetanus-diphtheria (every 10 years)
Avoid live vaccines if CD4 <200 (e.g., yellow fever, MMR, varicella — check with provider).

### 36. Is it safe to get the COVID-19 vaccine if I have HIV?
Yes. People with HIV should get the COVID-19 vaccine regardless of CD4 count, unless there is a specific contraindication. People with HIV were included in clinical trials, and the vaccine is safe and effective.

### 37. Can traditional medicine cure HIV?
No. There is no traditional or herbal medicine that cures HIV. Some traditional remedies can interfere with ART, making treatment less effective. Always tell your provider about any traditional medicines you are taking.

### 38. Why do I need to go to the clinic when I feel fine?
Regular clinic visits are important for:
- Monitoring viral load (to ensure ART is working)
- Checking for side effects (some are not noticeable at first)
- Screening for TB and other infections
- Refilling your ART prescription
- Checking your weight, blood pressure, and overall health
- Getting adherence support

### 39. What if I miss a clinic appointment?
Call the clinic to reschedule as soon as possible. Do not run out of medication. Most clinics will reschedule you within 1–2 weeks. If you miss multiple appointments, a community health worker may follow up with you. Missing appointments is common — do not be afraid to go back.

### 40. Can having HIV cause other health problems?
Yes. Untreated HIV weakens the immune system, leading to OIs. Even with well-controlled HIV, people with HIV have higher risks of:
- Cardiovascular disease
- Kidney disease
- Liver disease
- Some cancers (cervical, anal, Hodgkin lymphoma)
- Osteoporosis
- Diabetes
Regular check-ups and a healthy lifestyle help manage these risks.

## Prevention

### 41. What is the best way to prevent HIV?
Combination prevention:
- **Condoms** (male and female) — correct and consistent use
- **PrEP** — daily pill for HIV-negative people at risk
- **ART** — for people with HIV (U=U prevents transmission)
- **Circumcision** — reduces male HIV acquisition risk by ~60%
- **PEP** — within 72 hours after exposure
- **Avoid sharing needles** — use sterile needles and syringes
- **HIV testing** — know your status and your partner's

### 42. How effective are condoms at preventing HIV?
When used correctly and consistently, male condoms are 99% effective at preventing HIV. Female condoms are 95% effective. Effectiveness is reduced by incorrect or inconsistent use.

### 43. What is the difference between PrEP and PEP?

| | PrEP | PEP |
|---|---|---|
| **Before or after?** | Before exposure | After exposure |
| **Start** | Take daily | Within 72 hours |
| **Duration** | Ongoing | 28 days |
| **For** | HIV-negative people at ongoing risk | Anyone after a high-risk exposure |

### 44. If I am on PrEP and my partner is on ART with U=U, are we "doubly protected"?
Yes. When a person with HIV is undetectable (U=U) and their HIV-negative partner is on PrEP, the protection against HIV is maximized. This is sometimes called "biomedical prevention" or "PrEP + U=U" and provides near-absolute protection.

## Stigma and Discrimination

### 45. Can I be fired because I have HIV?
No. Nigeria's HIV Anti-Discrimination Act (2014) prohibits discrimination against people with HIV in employment, education, healthcare, housing, and public services. If you experience discrimination, you can report it to the National Agency for the Control of AIDS (NACA) or seek legal assistance.

### 46. Can I be denied healthcare because I have HIV?
No. Denying healthcare based on HIV status is illegal in Nigeria. Every person has the right to medical care regardless of their HIV status. If a healthcare provider refuses to treat you, report them to the facility management or the Ministry of Health.

### 47. Can I join the military or police if I have HIV?
Nigeria's military and police have fitness requirements, and HIV may affect eligibility for certain roles. Policies vary and may change. Check with the recruitment body for current requirements.

## HIV in the Nigerian Context

### 48. How common is HIV in Nigeria?
Nigeria has the fourth-largest HIV epidemic in the world (after South Africa, India, and Mozambique). The national prevalence is approximately 1.4% among adults. Prevalence is highest in the South-South zone (e.g., Akwa Ibom, Rivers) and lowest in the North-West.

### 49. What is NACA?
NACA is the **National Agency for the Control of AIDS** — the government body responsible for coordinating Nigeria's HIV response. State-level agencies (SACAs) coordinate at the state level. NACA provides policies, guidelines, and oversight for HIV services in Nigeria.

### 50. Where can I get free HIV treatment in Nigeria?
Free ART is available at public health facilities across Nigeria, supported by the Federal Government, PEPFAR, WHO, and other partners. You can find ART services at:
- General hospitals
- Primary healthcare centres (some)
- Dedicated HIV treatment clinics
- PEPFAR-supported sites
Ask at the nearest public hospital for the HIV/ART clinic.
